Actual Egg Timer

Privacy

Last updated 3 October 2026

Actual Egg Timer is a free app that I, Dan MacKinlay, make on my own as a personal project. It comes as a website and as an iPhone app.

In short

By default, nothing you put into Actual Egg Timer leaves your phone or your browser.

If you turn on Share my eggs, in Settings, the app sends me how each egg was cooked and how you said it came out, with a random number in place of your name. Turn it off and nothing more is sent. Delete what I’ve sent, in the same place, deletes everything that was.

What stays on your device

So that they work the same way next time, both apps keep:

  • your settings, such as the egg size, the units, your altitude, how much water you use, how you cool the eggs, and the language;
  • the cook in progress, so a running timer survives the page reloading or the app closing;
  • the eggs you rated: how each was cooked and how you said it came out, and what the app has learned from them;
  • how long your water took to boil, for each amount you have timed;
  • if you have turned sharing on: whether it is on, your random number and any you used before (so that deleting reaches all of them), and how many of your eggs have been sent.

On iPhone, these are kept in the app’s own storage on the phone. If you back up your iPhone, the backup includes them, as it does any app’s data. If you share, the iPhone app also makes a key in the phone’s secure chip (below), whose secret half never leaves the phone.

On the web, they are kept in your browser’s storage for this site. The website also keeps a copy of its own files (the page, its code, its words and its pictures) in your browser, so that it opens with no signal. That copy holds nothing about you, and is replaced when the site changes.

What sharing sends

For each egg you cook, including the eggs you rated before you turned sharing on:

  • the day you cooked it (a date, with no time of day);
  • the egg’s weight, whether you weighed it, measured it or picked a size, and for a size, whether the sizes were European or American;
  • how you cooked it: into cold or boiling water, how cold the egg was and whether that was the fridge, the room or a temperature you typed, the room temperature the app worked with, the temperature your water boils at, how long it took to boil and whether you timed that, how much water, how many eggs, whether the heat stayed on, and how you cooled them;
  • how you wanted the yolk, the time the app gave you, any change it made to that time on purpose (below), when the eggs came out and whether you said so, and how long the app counted for cooling;
  • your answers about the yolk and the white, and a probe thermometer reading if you gave one;
  • what the app expected you to say, and which version of its reckoning and its starting guesses it used;
  • which app it was (web or iPhone), its version, and the language and units you were reading it in;
  • your random number, and the egg’s place in the order you sent them.

The iPhone app also signs each egg it sends (below).

What is not sent: your name, an email address, an account, your location, your contacts, your device’s name or any of its identifiers, an advertising identifier, or anything from outside the app.

Two things in an egg say a little about where you are. The temperature your water boils at says roughly how high up you are. Your language, your units and whether you picked European or American egg sizes may suggest a part of the world. Nothing sent says more than that.

The random number is made on your device, from nothing about you, the first time you turn sharing on. It groups your eggs together, so the app can learn how one cook’s eggs differ from another’s, and it is what lets you delete them. After Start learning again, the app uses a new one, and your next eggs count as a new cook’s.

The time may move a few seconds. While sharing is on, the app sometimes moves the time it gives you by up to ten seconds either way, small enough to stay within “just right”. Cooks who all follow the app’s usual times teach it only about those times. A few seconds either side teaches it what happens near them. A small Learning mark beside the time says so while sharing is on.

On iPhone, the first time the app sends under a random number, it makes a key in the phone’s secure chip and asks Apple’s App Attest service to confirm that the key belongs to a genuine copy of the app on a real iPhone. That request goes to Apple and carries none of your eggs. It carries a scrambled form of your random number that cannot be turned back into it. After that, the app signs every egg it sends with the key. This lets me give eggs from the app more weight than eggs from anywhere else. If it does not work, your eggs are sent anyway.

What I keep, where, and for how long

I keep each egg exactly as it was sent, with only the details listed above. Anything else in a request is dropped. For the iPhone app I also keep, for each random number, the key’s public half and its name, a count of the eggs it has signed, whether that copy of the app came from the App Store, TestFlight or a developer’s build, and its build number. The code that receives eggs writes down no address, no device and no time.

Where. In Netlify’s storage for this site, in the United States (the region Netlify calls us-east-2).

How long. Until you delete them, and at most five years from the day each egg was cooked. The App Attest details for a random number go when its last egg does. If I stop making the app, I delete them all.

Who can see them. Only me, through my Netlify account, and Netlify, which stores them for me. I don’t publish anyone’s eggs or give them to anyone. To work out better first guesses, I copy the eggs to my own computer, never into the app’s public code, and delete that copy when I’m done. What I publish is the result, a handful of numbers about eggs in general, which every copy of the app starts from.

Your IP address. The code that receives eggs does not store it. Netlify’s request logs record it, with the time and the page asked for, for about a day (a week on paid plans). When you delete what you sent, the page asked for includes your random number. Netlify also counts requests by address to turn away floods. I don’t use these logs for anything. They are under Netlify’s own privacy policy.

Stopping and deleting

  • To stop: turn Share my eggs off in Settings. Nothing more is sent, and the time stays where the app would put it. What was already sent stays until you delete it.
  • To delete what you sent: Delete what I’ve sent, in Settings, deletes every egg your device has sent, under every random number it has used, with the App Attest details, and turns sharing off. If you are offline, the app keeps asking until the server confirms.
  • By email: write to forgetmyeggs@danmackinlay.name with your random number, and I’ll delete its eggs. I need the number, because nothing else tells me which eggs are yours. Neither app shows the number yet, so for now the button is the way to delete.
  • On your device: on iPhone, delete the app, and everything it kept goes with it. On the web, clear this site’s data in your browser’s settings. Delete what you sent first, because once the app and its random number are gone, I cannot tell which eggs were yours.
  • Start learning again, in Settings, deletes the eggs you rated and the boil times on your device, and keeps your settings. It does not delete what you sent.

The legal part, in plain words

This is a personal project, but the app is open to anyone, so I treat the eggs people share as the data protection law of the EU and the UK expects. Sharing is optional, and the timer works the same without it.

  • Who is responsible: I am, Dan MacKinlay, as a private person. Write to forgetmyeggs@danmackinlay.name about anything on this page. I live in Australia.
  • Why: to improve the first guesses every copy of the app starts from, and for nothing else. Nothing I do with your eggs decides anything about you.
  • On what basis: your consent, which you give by turning sharing on. You take it back for new eggs by turning sharing off, and for the eggs already sent by deleting them, with the button or by email. Taking it back doesn’t make what was done before it unlawful.
  • Who handles it for me: Netlify, Inc. hosts this site and stores the eggs, as my processor under the data processing agreement in its terms. Apple delivers the iPhone app and runs App Attest, under its own privacy policy.
  • Sent to the United States: the eggs are stored in the United States. Netlify, Inc. is certified under the EU–U.S. Data Privacy Framework (with its UK and Swiss parts), which the European Commission has found protects data adequately. Its data processing agreement adds the Commission’s standard contractual clauses as a fallback.
  • Your rights: you can ask me for a copy of your eggs, in a form another program can read, and ask me to correct them, delete them, stop using them for a while, or stop using them at all. You can take back your consent at any time. Write to me with your random number. If you think I have handled your eggs wrongly, you can complain to a data protection authority, such as the one in the country where you live. Please write to me first, so I can put it right.
  • Children: the app is not made for children. If you are under 16, please leave sharing off.
  • In Australia: the Privacy Act’s principles generally don’t apply to a private person with a free app like this one, but I follow this page all the same.

What I don’t do

  • No accounts and no sign-in.
  • No analytics, no advertising, no tracking and no cookies.
  • No code from other companies in either app: no libraries, kits or trackers. Both apps are built only from the code in the project’s public repository, on Apple’s and your browser’s own software. The part on this site that receives shared eggs uses Netlify’s own library for Netlify’s storage.
  • The website loads only its own files, from this site, the font included, so no font service learns that you visited.
  • Beyond the website loading itself, neither app connects to anything but this site, and then only to send or delete your eggs when you share. The one exception is App Attest, when the iPhone app first sends (above).

Both apps have links, in Help and to the code, that open in your browser only when you tap them.

Alarms and the Lock Screen

On iPhone, the alarm is a notification that your phone schedules itself, and the timer on the Lock Screen and in the Dynamic Island runs on your phone. Neither goes through any server, mine or Apple’s. On the web, the alarm is a sound the page plays.

Apple

If you have agreed, in your iPhone’s settings, to share analytics with app developers, Apple may pass me crash reports and overall numbers. If you send feedback through TestFlight, it comes to me. Both come from Apple, not from the app.
